The crust on a lava lake breaks along the same lines every time it cools.

Three places, one volcano.

The summit

It refills from below.

A fountain inside Halemaʻumaʻu, January 2021. The lake sits on top of a conduit, and the conduit is not a fixed thing — it opens, and the lake rises until it is lapping at the crater wall; it closes, and the whole surface drains away in hours.

That is why the summit is the instrument. Tiltmeters on the caldera rim read the ground swelling and settling as magma moves beneath it, days before anything reaches the surface.

The rift

It opened and stayed open.

Fissure 8, June 2018. Twenty-four cracks opened across the lower East Rift Zone that summer. One of them kept going, built its own cone, and fed a channel to the sea for three months.

While it ran, the summit lava lake drained and the caldera floor collapsed in stages — the same magma, leaving by a different door, forty kilometres away.

The sample

Pulled by hand.

Molten rock drawn out on a steel rod and dropped straight into water. The quench freezes the chemistry at the instant it left the flow, before the crystals have time to grow and rewrite it.

Read in a lab the same week, it says how hot the magma was and how far it had travelled. No instrument on the rim can answer that. Someone has to walk out and take it.

Field notes.

Two names for the same rock

Pāhoehoe runs smooth and skins over into rope. ʻAʻā tears itself into loose clinker that crunches underfoot and shreds boots. They are the same melt; the difference is how fast it was moving and how much gas it had left when it cooled.

Why the steam matters

A plume is not the eruption. It is groundwater meeting something still hot enough to boil it, which means the crack it is rising through has not closed. Mapping plumes maps the part of the system that is still open.

A century on the rim

The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory has sat on the edge of this caldera since 1912. Its archive is the reason a page like this can exist at all: more than a hundred years of photographs taken as part of the job, placed in the public domain the day they were filed.

What the record is for

Eruptions are compared against their predecessors. A frame from 1959 and a frame from 2018 are evidence in the same argument, which is why each one carries a date, a place and a name, and why none of them has been improved.
